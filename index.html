Infrastructures On Ice

A geographic archive of American intervention in Greenland

Greenland has been subject to the strategic occupational whims of the United States for the past century. During the Cold War, the U.S. military constructed a constellation of infrastructures across the island: base camps, airstrips, research sites, radar stations, seismic outposts, ice tunnels, and a nuclear power plant. Today, the vast majority of these architectural interventions remain abandoned on and in the Greenland ice sheet. The United States continues to deny legal obligation for remediation of military waste and its associated contaminations under Article XI of the 1951 Defense Agreement, which states that “any areas or facilities made available to the Government of the United States of America under this Agreement need not be left in the condition in which they were at the time they were thus made available.”

The following digital atlas spatializes archival documentation of infrastructural interventions by the United States in Greenland from the twentieth century to the present. Maps, photographs, diagrams, plans, and declassified documents illustrate how military and ecological violence are continually inscribed onto Greenland's arctic landscape.

Heat thematically links Cold War militarization, glacial engineering, nuclear infrastructure, and contemporary climate change. Camp Century was a secret U.S. Army base that was built in 1959, including a nuclear power plant under the guise of scientific research. The camp was built under the ice sheet, relying on the ice to act as a thermal sink for its nuclear reactors. As shifting ice collapsed several tunnels, the base and its radioactive waste were abandoned in 1967 with minimal decommissioning, assumed to remain frozen in time. Recent studies, however, show that climate change induced ice sheet melting is expected to resurface Camp Century's nuclear waste within the century, posing a toxic environmental hazard whose responsibility falls on Denmark. Yet, ironically, the United States military alone emits more greenhouse gases than the entire country of Denmark. Camp Century is just one site that collapses temperature across spatial and temporal scales to destabilize notions of permanence and stability.

The physical traces of U.S. militarism that remain embedded in Greenland highlight tensions between the ice—vast, dynamic, deep time—and its imposing architectures—discrete, static, short-lived. This infrastructure-oriented atlas contextualizes the United States' continued interest in acquiring Greenland as a strategic territory for military expansion and resource extraction, grounding this imperialist ideology in concrete interventions and their enduring material consequences.
